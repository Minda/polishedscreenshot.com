Polished Screenshot

Every screenshot is a decision

Thirteen guidelines for documenting software that changes faster than you can write about it. Each one says what it costs you, and names the two moments when you should ignore it and take the picture.

Minda Myers mindamyers.com github.com/Minda About a 10-minute read

For your agent

Point your agent at this page

Paste the first prompt into your agent along with your documentation. Add the second after it to have the agent make the changes itself.

1. Review my documentation

Read this page. It sets out thirteen guidelines for deciding which screenshots to keep in documentation. Review the documentation I give you against them. For each screenshot, say whether to keep it, replace it with text, or cut it, and name the guideline.

2. Then make the changes

Then make the changes yourself, writing or updating the documentation as needed. Run the page's six-question check for each step, date every screenshot you keep, and list each one with the exception behind it.

Note for agents: every guideline, tradeoff and exception is in this page's HTML. If you are reading the rendered page, turn on Text version (top right) first.

The software you're documenting redesigns itself every few weeks now. AI products are the worst offenders, but it's spreading. A button moves. A panel gets a new name. The settings page splits in two. Every screenshot you publish is a small debt. It comes due at the next redesign, and nothing tells you when. You find out when a reader emails to say the button isn't there.

So: thirteen guidelines. Each one says what you give up by following it, plus two situations where following it would make the document worse. Hit one of those, take the screenshot. The point isn't zero screenshots. The point is that every screenshot in your document is one you decided to take.

Every essential action, interface label, prerequisite, warning, and expected result has to be available outside the screenshot.

The thirteen

Each guideline, what it costs, and when to break it

Guideline 1

Crop to the minimum

Screenshot only the part of the screen that shows the thing. The test: if the crop is so tight the reader can't tell where they are, go one step wider. Stop when they can find the part of the app they need to work in.

loses: orientation
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Orientation. A cropped toggle looks like every other toggle. The reader gets the control but not where it lives, and they lose the "am I even in the right place" reassurance a wider shot gives them.

When not to follow it

  • First screenshot in the document. The reader needs to establish they're in the right application before anything else works.
  • The control is generic. An unlabeled toggle, a plain text field. Only the stuff around it tells the reader which one you mean.

Guideline 2

Write the path as text

Write the route the way you'd say it. Settings → Members → Invite

A rename still costs you an edit. But it's a text edit, and text is searchable.

loses: pointing
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

The thing screenshots are best at. Pointing. The reader still has to locate each item at each level. Paths also assume every step has a visible label, which falls apart on icon-only sidebars and overflow menus.

When not to follow it

  • A step in the path has no label. You can't write what has no name. Use an inline glyph (guideline 4) or a tight crop.
  • The path runs deeper than about four levels. Readers lose the thread. A deep link or the product's own search gets them there better.

Guideline 3

Name the control the way the interface does

"The Invite members button." Exactly that string. One sentence serves three audiences: sighted readers, screen reader users hearing the same words announced, and anyone hitting Ctrl+F.

loses: position
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Position. Names repeat. "Edit" might show up six times on one page and the callout gives the reader no way to pick the right one.

When not to follow it

  • The name appears more than once on the screen.
  • The visible label and the accessible name don't match. Happens constantly with icon buttons whose tooltip says one thing and whose aria-label says another. The reader ends up hunting for text that isn't on screen anywhere.

Guideline 4

Put the icon in the sentence

"Click the ⚙ icon." Small, scales with the text, no screenshot.

loses: locationfidelity risk
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Location again. You've identified the icon, not where it sits. There's a fidelity risk too: your glyph isn't the vendor's glyph, and a near-match confuses people more than no picture would.

When not to follow it

  • The vendor's icon is custom and nothing standard comes close. Use a tiny crop.
  • Several similar icons sit next to each other. A toolbar of gears. Then position is the information, not shape.

Guideline 5

Hand it to the product's own search

"Press ⌘K, type invite."

This survives any redesign, because the vendor maintains the mapping between the word and the place. You stop owning that problem. Best durability on this list.

loses: precisionbest durability
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Precision. Search results vary by tenant and plan. The query might return five hits with nothing telling the reader which one.

When not to follow it

  • Your word isn't their word. If your team calls it one thing and the vendor calls it another, the search comes back empty and the reader concludes the feature doesn't exist.
  • Admin settings. They frequently aren't in product search at all. Check before you rely on it.

Guideline 6

Deep link to the exact page

Link straight to the settings page instead of describing the walk. Put the text path underneath it as a fallback, every time.

URLs change. When they do it's a text edit, which is the trade you signed up for.

loses: navigational knowledge
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Navigational knowledge. The reader gets there once and learns nothing about getting back without you.

When not to follow it

  • The page is permission-gated. The failure isn't clean. It's a silent redirect to some dashboard, or a 403 the reader reads as their own mistake.
  • The reader will come back often. Teaching the route beats the shortcut.

Guideline 7

Anything they type goes in a code block

Prompts, values, URLs: code block. Never screenshot a string somebody has to type.

Show the paste target only when the input field is unlabeled or sits among several that look the same. Not "when it feels helpful." Three authors will resolve that three ways and the document ends up with no rule at all.

loses: destination
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

The destination. The reader has the string, not the box it goes in.

When not to follow it

  • The input field is unlabeled, or there are three that look the same.
  • Pasting does something unexpected. Reformats the text, splits it across fields, fires off a search.

Guideline 8

End with something the reader can check

Replace the end-state screenshot with something countable. "Confirm 3 members are listed."

loses: "does this look right?"
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

The answer to "does this look right?" A checklist only works for states the reader can already find and name. Somebody who can't locate the member list can't count three members in it.

When not to follow it

  • Success is visual, not countable. A chart rendered. A layout shifted. A badge appeared.
  • The action is destructive or hard to undo. Then the reader needs to verify before committing, not after.

Guideline 9

Use the vendor's screenshot, and name the section

Link to the vendor's article rather than reproducing their interface. Name the section you're pointing at, because a named section survives a broken anchor. The reader can search the page for the words.

loses: control
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Control. Their crop, their plan tier, their configuration, their link rot. It also pulls the reader out of your document into content written for somebody else.

When not to follow it

  • Their screenshot shows a tier or configuration you don't have. SSO differences, disabled features, admin-only controls. Check this every single time. It's the common case, not the edge case.
  • Their page sits behind a login, is region-gated, or shows a control your readers can't access.

Guideline 10

One decision table instead of four screenshots

Four states, four screenshots? Make it a table. If you see A, do X. If you see B, do Y.

loses: recognition
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Recognition. "If you see A" assumes the reader can already spot A. When the states look alike and resist description, the table can't do the work.

When not to follow it

  • The states are hard to put in words. Subtle visual differences. Error states that look nearly identical.
  • There are only two states. Then a table is more overhead than a sentence.

Guideline 11

A field table, with one sentence saying where the form is

Swap the annotated form screenshot for a table. Field, What to enter, Why.

Open every field table with one sentence saying where the form lives. Not optional. Without it you've handed the reader answers to a question they can't find.

loses: the shape of the form
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

The shape of the form. Tables flatten sections, grouping, and order. They also hide which fields are required unless you add a column for it.

When not to follow it

  • The form has conditional logic. Field B only appears when field A is set a certain way. Tables handle that badly.
  • The form is long enough that people lose their place. A screenshot works as a map.

Guideline 12

Give every screenshot a date the reader can find

Every screenshot needs a date: when it was taken, or when it was last checked against the live interface. The date doesn't have to sit on the image. Four places work. Pick one per document and stick with it.

  • In the caption. Captured 14 Aug 2026 Visible to everyone, costs you a line under every image.
  • In the alt text or the description, after whatever the step didn't already say. Alt text for a simple image; the longer description guideline 13 calls for when the image is complex. Screen reader users hear it, Ctrl+F finds it, and the page stays clean.
  • Once at the top, plus a tag on anything revised since. Interface verified 14 Aug 2026 at the top of the document, then Revised 3 Oct 2026 only on the screenshots that changed after that.

Without a date, staleness is invisible. The reader can't tell if they're looking at last week's interface or last spring's, so whatever trust they put in it is unearned.

loses: cleanliness, or visibility
What it costs, and when to break it ▾

What's lost

Depends where you put it. A caption costs visual cleanliness, and one thing less obvious: a visible date invites people to dismiss a screenshot as old even when it's still correct. Alt text or a description avoids both, and hides the date from sighted readers, which is its own problem. The top-of-document line is the cleanest, and it only works if you actually tag the revisions.

When not to follow it

  • The screenshot shows something version-stable. A generic OS file dialog. Dating it implies more churn than actually exists.
  • The publishing system already stamps the document with a visible last-updated date, and every screenshot was checked at that update. Then a per-screenshot date repeats what the reader can already see. The moment one screenshot is older than that stamp, it needs its own tag.

Guideline 13

Alt text carries only what the step doesn't already say

Write alt text for the part of the image the step text hasn't covered. Nothing else.

Here's the useful part. If you sit down to write that alt text and there's nothing left to say, you've just learned that you don't need the screenshot.

loses nothing; that's the point
When to break it ▾

When not to follow it

  • The image is complex. A full dashboard, a chart. The delta is too big for one alt string, so write a longer description near the image instead. Alt text is the wrong container.
  • The delta is an error message or a status string. Put it in the prose. Then the screenshot might disappear as well, which is the outcome you're after.

For any screenshot that stays

Two tests. Use your hand.

Cover the image with your hand or a couple of fingers. Actually do it. Then:

  1. Read the alt text by itself. With the image blocked out, does it tell you what the image was trying to convey? Not an inventory of what's in it. What it's there to show. If you'd have to lift your hand to finish the step, the alt text isn't done.
  2. Now read the text around it. Hand still down. Does the alt text support and add to what's already on the page, or does it just repeat it? If it repeats, trim it to the part the step doesn't say (guideline 13). If nothing is left after trimming, the screenshot goes too.

Try it on a step you're writing

Do you need a screenshot here?

Six questions, each built from one guideline's exceptions. Answer for the one step in front of you. Most steps end at "skip it."

Answer each in order. The first "yes" decides.

  1. Is this the first screenshot in the document? Yes: take it, cropped wide enough to show the application. Guideline 1.
  2. Does the control's name appear more than once on the screen, or not match its visible label, or have no label at all? Yes: take a tight crop, or use an inline glyph if the icon is standard. Guidelines 3 and 4.
  3. Will the reader paste or type into a field that is unlabeled or sits among lookalikes, or does pasting do something unexpected? Yes: show the paste target. Guideline 7.
  4. Is success visual rather than countable, or is the action destructive or hard to undo? Yes: take the end-state screenshot. Guideline 8.
  5. Are you describing states that are hard to tell apart in words? Yes: screenshot the states instead of a decision table. Guideline 10.
  6. Is the form conditional, or long enough that people lose their place? Yes: one screenshot as a map, plus the field table. Guideline 11.

All six "no": skip the screenshot. Write the path (2), name the control (3), hand off to search (5) or deep link (6), and finish with something the reader can check (8). If a screenshot does stay, give it a findable date (12) and write alt text for the delta only (13).
